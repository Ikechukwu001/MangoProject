## Malaria

**Malaria** is a disease caused by **Plasmodium parasites**, spread by the bite of the **female Anopheles mosquito**. In Nigeria, ***Plasmodium falciparum*** is the most common and the most dangerous type.

### Symptoms

- **Fever** (often with chills and sweating)
- Headache, body and joint pains
- Tiredness and weakness
- Nausea and vomiting
- In **children**, fever with poor feeding and irritability

### Danger signs of severe malaria

- Convulsions, drowsiness or **coma**
- **Difficulty breathing**
- **Persistent vomiting**, unable to drink
- **Jaundice** or very pale skin
- **Dark urine**
- **Very low urine output**

Severe malaria is an **emergency**. **Refer at once.**

## Diagnosis first

Many fevers are **not malaria**. Wherever possible, **confirm** malaria with a **rapid diagnostic test (RDT)** or **microscopy** before giving treatment. This avoids wasting drugs, slows resistance, and ensures that other illnesses are not missed.

## Treatment of uncomplicated malaria

The national guidelines recommend **artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT)** as **first-line** treatment.

| Combination | Notes |
|---|---|
| Artemether-lumefantrine (AL) | Tablets taken twice a day for **3 days**, usually taken **with food or a fatty drink** to help absorption. Dose by weight band |
| Artesunate-amodiaquine (AS-AQ) | Taken once a day for 3 days. Nausea, vomiting and weakness may occur |
| Other ACTs (for example dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine, artesunate-pyronaridine) | Used as alternatives in some programmes |

### Why a combination?

- The **artemisinin** part acts **fast** and reduces the number of parasites.
- The **partner drug** acts **longer** and clears the rest.
- Two drugs with different actions make it **harder for resistant parasites** to survive.

**Monotherapy** (a single artemisinin drug alone) is **discouraged**, because it promotes resistance.

### Older drugs

**Chloroquine** and **sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP)** are no longer used for treatment of uncomplicated *P. falciparum* malaria in Nigeria, because of resistance. **SP** is still used for **preventive treatment in pregnancy**. Quinine remains a treatment for severe malaria and in some other cases, in specified circumstances.

### Counselling

- **Complete all doses**, even after the fever goes.
- Take **with food** or a **fatty drink** (for artemether-lumefantrine).
- If the patient **vomits within 30 minutes** (or as advised), the dose may need to be repeated. Ask the pharmacist.
- **Paracetamol** may be used for fever. **Do not** use aspirin in children.
- Drink plenty of fluids.
- **Return** if there is no improvement after 2 to 3 days, or if the fever returns or worsens.
- **Pregnancy:** the choice of drug depends on the stage of pregnancy. Ask the pharmacist.
- **Never share** antimalarials, and buy only from **approved** pharmacies, because fake drugs are common.
- Possible **side effects:** nausea, vomiting, dizziness, headache, weakness. Report rash, jaundice or fainting.

## Treatment of severe malaria

- **Injectable artesunate** is the preferred treatment, given in **hospital**, followed by a full course of an oral ACT.
- Other options include **artemether** injection or **quinine** injection when artesunate is not available.
- Supportive care includes treating **convulsions**, **low blood sugar**, **anaemia** and **dehydration**.
- A child with danger signs needing **pre-referral treatment** (for example rectal artesunate) should be **sent to hospital quickly**.

## Prevention

| Measure | Notes |
|---|---|
| Insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) | Sleep under a net every night. Mend holes |
| Indoor residual spraying | Reduces mosquitoes in the home |
| Environmental control | Remove standing water and clear drains and bush |
| Intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy (IPTp) | Sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine given at antenatal visits, **under supervision**, as the national guidelines say |
| Seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC) | Given to young children in areas with seasonal malaria, in some programmes |
| Chemoprophylaxis for travellers | Drugs such as atovaquone-proguanil, doxycycline or mefloquine, chosen by the pharmacist or doctor |
| Malaria vaccine | Introduced in some countries for children, according to national policy |

**Pregnant women and young children are at greatest risk**, so early antenatal care and the use of nets are important.

## Resistance and its prevention

- Use **ACTs** and avoid monotherapy.
- **Confirm the diagnosis** before treating.
- **Complete the course.**
- Use **quality-assured** medicines. **Fake and substandard** antimalarials reduce cure rates and increase resistance.
- **Report** suspect products to the pharmacist and to NAFDAC.

## The technician's role

- **Ask** about fever duration, other symptoms, age and pregnancy.
- **Recognise danger signs** and refer.
- **Do not** sell antimalarials without a prescription or confirmed diagnosis where policy requires it, and ask the pharmacist.
- **Check** the dose by weight band, as printed on the pack.
- **Counsel** clearly on timing, food, vomiting and finishing the course.
