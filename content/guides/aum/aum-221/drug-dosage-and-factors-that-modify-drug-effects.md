## Why doses are not the same for everyone

The **dose** printed in a reference is for an **average healthy adult**. People are different, so the same dose can be **too strong for one person and too weak for another**. The dose and the choice of drug must fit the patient.

## Factors that modify the effect of a drug

| Factor | How it changes the effect |
|---|---|
| Age | Babies and the elderly handle drugs more slowly. Children need doses based on age or weight |
| Body weight | A heavier person needs a larger dose, a lighter person a smaller one |
| Sex | Differences in body fat, hormones and size can alter response. Pregnancy and breast-feeding need special care |
| Pregnancy and breast-feeding | Many drugs reach the baby. Some cause harm to the unborn baby or the infant |
| Disease (pathology) | Kidney or liver disease slows removal. Heart failure and shock change absorption and distribution |
| Genetic factors | Inherited differences in enzymes can make a person respond more or less strongly |
| Rate of elimination | A slow rate means drugs build up. A fast rate means they are shorter-acting |
| Dosage form | A solution acts faster than a tablet, and a modified-release tablet acts longer |
| Route of administration | Intravenous acts fastest, oral slowest |
| Time of administration | Before or after food, morning or night, can alter effect |
| Medication errors | Wrong drug, dose, time or patient |
| Patient compliance | Missed doses or extra doses |
| Tolerance | Repeated use may reduce effect |
| Physiological variables | Stress, temperature, hydration, and acid levels in the gut |
| Drug interactions | Other drugs, food, herbs or alcohol |

## Age

**Babies and children**

- Newborns have **immature liver and kidneys**, so drugs build up. Doses must be **small and carefully calculated**.
- Children are **not small adults**. They need doses by **age or weight**, and special forms such as syrups.
- Some drugs are **not suitable** for children, for example **aspirin** under 16 years because of Reye's syndrome, and some drugs affect growth or teeth, such as **tetracyclines**.

**The elderly**

- The kidneys and liver work **less well**, and body water is lower, so drugs stay **longer**.
- They often take **many medicines**, so **interactions** are more likely.
- They may be **more sensitive** to side effects such as drowsiness, confusion and falls.
- Doses may be **lower**, and the pharmacist checks them.

## Weight, sex, pregnancy and breast-feeding

- A dose based on **weight** (mg per kg) is often used for children and for some drugs in adults.
- **Pregnancy:** many drugs can reach the unborn baby. The risk depends on the drug and the stage of pregnancy. **Never** advise a pregnant woman to take a medicine without checking with the pharmacist.
- **Breast-feeding:** many drugs pass into milk. The pharmacist decides which are safe.

## Disease

- **Kidney disease:** drugs removed by the kidneys build up, so doses are reduced.
- **Liver disease:** drugs broken down by the liver build up, and some are best avoided.
- **Heart failure or shock:** less blood reaches organs, so absorption and removal change.
- **Gut disease or diarrhoea:** may reduce absorption.

## Genetics

Some people have **inherited differences** in the enzymes that break down certain drugs. They may **respond very strongly or very weakly**, or have **unusual reactions** (idiosyncrasy). This is **pharmacogenetics**.

## Dosage form, route and time

- The **dosage form** changes the **speed and amount** absorbed.
- The **route** changes speed. See the lesson on routes of administration.
- **Time of administration:** some drugs work best **before meals** and some **with or after meals** to reduce stomach upset. Some are taken **at night** (for example some sedatives) or **in the morning** (for example steroids, to match natural hormone levels).

## Interactions, tolerance, errors and compliance

- **Drug interactions** can increase or decrease effects. Food, herbs and alcohol can interact too.
- **Tolerance** means that, after repeated use, a larger dose is needed for the same effect.
- **Medication errors** (wrong drug, dose or patient) can cause harm. Careful checking prevents them.
- **Poor compliance** reduces effect, while **taking extra doses** increases the risk of harm.

## Working out a child's dose

A **child's dose** should ideally come from a **reference** or **the prescriber**, in mg per kg or by age band. When a rule is used, it is a rough guide, and the pharmacist decides.

### Young's rule (by age)

```
child dose = age ÷ (age + 12) × adult dose
```

**Example.** *The adult dose is 300 mg. The child is 6 years old.*

1. Fraction = 6 ÷ (6 + 12) = 6 ÷ 18 = 1/3
2. Dose = 300 × 1/3 = **100 mg**

### By weight

```
dose = dose per kg × weight in kg
```

**Example.** *The dose is 10 mg per kg and the child weighs 15 kg.*

- Dose = 10 × 15 = **150 mg**

### Clark's rule (by weight, using the adult as 70 kg)

```
child dose = weight (kg) ÷ 70 × adult dose
```

**Example.** *The adult dose is 700 mg and the child weighs 21 kg.*

- Dose = 21 ÷ 70 × 700 = **210 mg**

These rules are simple guides. See the lesson on calculation of dosages in BDT 253 for more examples.

## What a technician should do

- **Ask** the age, weight, pregnancy or breast-feeding status, and other medicines.
- **Check** the dose against a reference, especially for children and the elderly.
- **Tell the pharmacist** about anything unusual: kidney or liver disease, allergy, many medicines, or a very young or old patient.
- **Counsel** the patient about timing, food and alcohol.
