## What is a route of administration?

The **route** is the **way a drug enters the body**. The choice depends on the **drug**, the **speed needed**, the **condition and age of the patient**, and **convenience and cost**.

## The main routes

| Route | How it is given | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Oral | By mouth, swallowed | Tablets, capsules, syrups |
| Sublingual and buccal | Under the tongue or in the cheek | Glyceryl trinitrate tablets for angina |
| Rectal | Into the rectum | Suppositories, enemas |
| Parenteral | By injection | Intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous, intradermal |
| Inhalation | Breathed into the lungs | Asthma inhalers, nebulisers, oxygen |
| Instillation | Drops into the eye, ear or nose | Eye drops, ear drops, nasal drops |
| Topical or external | On the skin or mucous membrane | Creams, ointments, lotions |
| Transdermal | Through the skin into the blood | Patches |
| Vaginal | Into the vagina | Pessaries, vaginal creams |

## Oral route

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Simple, convenient, cheap | Slower action |
| Painless, can be self-administered | Needs a patient who can swallow and who is conscious |
| Safe, with no need for sterile equipment | Some drugs are destroyed by stomach acid or enzymes |
| Wide range of forms | First-pass effect in the liver reduces the amount reaching the blood |
| Easy to stop or to treat overdose by pumping the stomach in some cases | Food and vomiting may affect absorption, and some drugs irritate the stomach |

## Parenteral routes (injection)

The drug **bypasses the gut**. Injections must be **sterile**, and given by trained staff, with **clean technique**.

| Route | Where | Speed | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intravenous (IV) | Into a vein | **Fastest**, 100% bioavailability | Used in emergencies and for large volumes. Risk of infection, and of reactions if given too fast. Cannot be recalled once given |
| Intramuscular (IM) | Into a muscle | Fairly fast | Can be used for oily or depot injections. Painful. Not suitable for patients on anticoagulants |
| Subcutaneous (SC) | Under the skin | Slower and steady | Used for insulin and some vaccines. Small volumes |
| Intradermal (ID) | Into the skin layer | Slow | Used for tests and some vaccines, such as BCG |

**Advantages of injection:** fast action, useful if the patient cannot swallow or is unconscious, precise dose, avoids first-pass.
**Disadvantages:** pain, risk of infection, needs trained staff and sterile equipment, cannot be reversed easily, and costs more.

## Sublingual, buccal and rectal

- **Sublingual** (under the tongue): fast, avoids the liver. Used in angina and some emergencies. Do not swallow.
- **Rectal:** suitable for patients who are vomiting, unconscious or children. Partly avoids the liver. Absorption can be irregular. Some patients dislike it.

## Inhalation

The drug is breathed in as a **gas, vapour, aerosol or fine powder**.

- **Fast** action in the lungs, with **small doses** and **fewer side effects** in the rest of the body.
- Used for **asthma** and **COPD**. Good **technique** is essential. A **spacer** helps.
- Oxygen and **anaesthetic gases** are also inhaled.

## Instillation

Drops are put into the **eye, ear or nose** for local effect.

- **Wash hands** first.
- **Do not touch** the tip to the eye, ear or nose.
- Eye drops are usually **discarded after about 4 weeks** once opened, or as the label says.
- Wait several minutes between **different** eye drops.

## Topical (external) and transdermal

- **Topical** products act **on the skin** or **mucous membranes**: creams, ointments, lotions, pastes.
- **Transdermal patches** release a drug **slowly through the skin into the blood**, for example for hormones, pain and nicotine replacement. Rotate the site, and do not cut patches.
- Absorption through damaged or thin skin is **greater**, so extra care is needed, especially in infants.

## Choosing a route

| Situation | Suitable route |
|---|---|
| Mild illness, patient can swallow | Oral |
| Emergency, or a very fast effect needed | Intravenous |
| Patient vomiting, unconscious or a young child | Rectal or injection |
| Asthma attack | Inhaled |
| Skin infection | Topical |
| Angina attack | Sublingual |
| Long-term hormone or pain treatment | Transdermal patch or depot injection |

## Order of speed of onset

From fastest to slowest, roughly: **intravenous → inhalation → sublingual → intramuscular → subcutaneous → rectal → oral → topical or transdermal**. The order can vary with the drug and form.

## Counselling points

- **Oral:** swallow with water, follow instructions on food, and do not crush modified-release tablets.
- **Inhaled:** learn the technique, use a spacer if advised, and rinse the mouth after steroid inhalers.
- **Eye, ear and nose drops:** wash hands, do not touch the tip, and do not share.
- **Topical:** wash hands, apply a thin layer, and avoid broken skin and the eyes unless told.
- **Injections:** only trained staff should give them, with new, sterile needles each time. Dispose of sharps safely.
