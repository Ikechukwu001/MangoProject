## The journey of a medicine

After a medicine is taken, it goes through four steps, often called **ADME**:

```
Absorption → Distribution → Metabolism → Excretion
```

The study of these steps is called **pharmacokinetics**. The curriculum also includes **receptor affinity**, which links the drug to its effect.

## 1. Absorption

**Absorption** is the movement of the drug **from the place where it is given into the blood**.

| Route | Absorption |
|---|---|
| Oral (swallowed) | Through the stomach and mainly the small intestine. Slower, and some drug is lost |
| Injection into a vein (intravenous) | None needed. The drug is in the blood at once |
| Injection into muscle or under the skin | Fairly quick, depending on the blood supply |
| Rectal | Partly avoids the liver, useful if the patient is vomiting |
| Under the tongue (sublingual) | Fast, direct into the blood, avoids the liver |
| Skin (transdermal) | Slow and steady through patches |
| Inhaled | Fast in the lungs |

### Factors that affect oral absorption

- **How well the drug dissolves** and its form: solutions are absorbed faster than tablets.
- **Acid or alkali conditions** in the stomach and gut.
- **Food**: it can slow or reduce absorption of some drugs, and increase that of others.
- **Gut movement**: diarrhoea reduces absorption, and slow emptying delays it.
- **Other drugs**, such as antacids binding to tetracyclines.
- **Disease** of the gut.

### First-pass effect and bioavailability

Blood from the gut passes through the **liver first**. The liver may break down much of the drug before it reaches the rest of the body. This is the **first-pass effect**.

**Bioavailability** is the **percentage of the dose that reaches the blood** in an active form.

```
bioavailability (%) = amount reaching the blood ÷ dose given × 100
```

**Example.** *A 100 mg oral dose gives 60 mg in the blood.*

- Bioavailability = 60 ÷ 100 × 100 = **60%**

An intravenous dose has a bioavailability of **100%**.

## 2. Distribution

**Distribution** is the **spread of the drug from the blood into the body**. It depends on:

- **blood flow**: organs with a good supply, such as the heart, liver, kidney and brain, receive drug first;
- **how easily the drug passes through membranes**, which depends on its size and whether it dissolves in fat or water;
- **binding to blood proteins** such as **albumin**. A drug bound to protein is **inactive and cannot leave the blood**. Only the **free** drug acts. A drug can be pushed off the protein by another drug, which can raise the free level and cause toxicity;
- **barriers**: the **blood-brain barrier** protects the brain, and the **placenta** lets many drugs reach the unborn baby. Many drugs also pass into **breast milk**, so the pharmacist must check these in pregnancy and breast-feeding.

## 3. Receptor affinity

Most drugs act by **binding to receptors**, which are special molecules on or in cells. This is like a **key fitting a lock**.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Receptor | A cell molecule to which a drug or natural chemical binds |
| Affinity | How **strongly** a drug is attracted to and binds with its receptor |
| Efficacy | The ability of the drug, once bound, to **produce an effect** |
| Agonist | A drug that **binds and activates** the receptor, producing an effect |
| Antagonist | A drug that **binds and blocks** the receptor without activating it |

**Examples.** Salbutamol is an **agonist** at receptors in the airways, so the airways open. A beta-blocker is an **antagonist** at heart receptors and slows the heart.

Some drugs do not use receptors. For example, **antacids** neutralise acid, and some **antibiotics** attack parts of germs.

## 4. Metabolism

**Metabolism** is the **chemical change** of a drug in the body, mainly by **enzymes in the liver**. It usually makes the drug **more water-soluble and less active**, so that the kidneys can remove it. The products are **metabolites**.

- Some drugs are changed into **active** metabolites. A **prodrug** is inactive until metabolised.
- **Enzyme inducers** (for example rifampicin and some antiepileptics) **speed up** metabolism, so other drugs may be weaker.
- **Enzyme inhibitors** (for example some antifungals and erythromycin) **slow down** metabolism, so other drugs may be stronger and more toxic.
- Metabolism is **slower in newborns, the elderly and people with liver disease**, so doses may need to be changed.

## 5. Excretion

**Excretion** is the **removal** of the drug and its metabolites from the body.

| Route | Notes |
|---|---|
| Kidneys (urine) | The main route. Poor kidney function means the drug builds up |
| Bile and faeces | Some drugs pass into the bile, and may be reabsorbed |
| Lungs | Gases and volatile drugs, such as anaesthetics |
| Sweat, saliva, tears | Small amounts |
| Breast milk | Small amounts, but important for the infant |

### Half-life

The **half-life (t½)** is the **time taken for the amount of drug in the blood to fall by half**.

**Example.** *A drug has a half-life of 4 hours. 100 mg is in the blood.*

| Time | Number of half-lives | Amount left |
|---|---|---|
| 0 h | 0 | 100 mg |
| 4 h | 1 | 50 mg |
| 8 h | 2 | 25 mg |
| 12 h | 3 | 12.5 mg |

A drug with a **long half-life** can be taken less often, and a drug with a **short half-life** may need frequent dosing. After about **5 half-lives**, a drug is almost completely removed.

## Why this matters

- The route and form decide **how fast the drug acts**.
- Food, other drugs and disease change the **dose needed**.
- **Kidney or liver disease**, age and pregnancy affect safe doses, so a pharmacist checks these.
- The **half-life** decides how often a medicine is given.
