## Sources of medicines

| Source | Examples |
|---|---|
| Plants | **Morphine** and codeine (opium poppy), **quinine** (cinchona bark), **digoxin** (foxglove), **atropine** (belladonna), **artemisinin** (*Artemisia annua*, the source of artemether and artesunate) |
| Animals | **Heparin** (from pig intestine), **insulin** (originally from pig and cattle pancreas), **antivenoms and antisera** (from animal blood), **cod liver oil**, some **hormones** and enzymes |
| Minerals | **Magnesium sulfate** and magnesium trisilicate, **kaolin**, **zinc oxide**, **iron salts**, **sodium chloride**, calamine |
| Microorganisms | **Penicillin** (from a mould, *Penicillium*), **streptomycin** (from soil bacteria, *Streptomyces*), many other antibiotics |
| Chemical synthesis | **Paracetamol**, **sulfonamides**, **ciprofloxacin**, **aspirin**, many modern drugs made completely in the laboratory |
| Semi-synthesis | **Amoxicillin** and ampicillin (from penicillin), many steroids from plant or animal starting materials |
| Biotechnology (genetic engineering) | **Human insulin** made by bacteria or yeast, and many **vaccines** and **biological medicines** |

### Natural, semi-synthetic and synthetic

- A **natural** medicine is taken from a plant, animal, mineral or microbe, and may be purified.
- A **semi-synthetic** medicine is made by **changing a natural substance** chemically, often to improve its action or stability.
- A **synthetic** medicine is **made entirely from simple chemicals** in the laboratory.

### Crude drugs and pure drugs

- A **crude drug** is the plant or animal material itself, for example dried leaves or bark.
- A **pure drug** is the active substance **isolated** from it, for example morphine from opium.
- Pure drugs have a **measurable, consistent dose**, which is why most modern medicines are pure drugs.

### Herbal and traditional medicines

Many people use **herbal medicines**. They may be useful, but the amount of active substance can **vary**, there may be **side effects** and **interactions**, and many have not been tested. A technician should record that a patient uses herbal products and tell the pharmacist.

## Branches of pharmacology

| Branch | What it studies |
|---|---|
| Pharmacokinetics | **What the body does to the drug**: absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion |
| Pharmacodynamics | **What the drug does to the body**: its effects and how it works |
| Pharmacotherapeutics (therapeutics) | The **use of drugs to treat disease** |
| Toxicology | The **harmful effects of drugs and poisons**, and how to treat poisoning |
| Pharmacognosy | Drugs from **plant and animal sources** |
| Clinical pharmacology | The study of drugs **in people**, including how to use them safely |
| Chemotherapy | The use of drugs to **kill or stop** germs, parasites or cancer cells, with little harm to the patient |
| Pharmacogenetics | How **inherited differences** affect a person's response to drugs |
| Pharmacovigilance | **Monitoring** and **reporting** the safety of medicines after they are on the market |
| Posology | The study of **doses** |

**Memory aid.** Pharmaco**kinetics** is about the **movement** of the drug through the body (what the body does to the drug). Pharmaco**dynamics** is about the **drug's action** (what the drug does to the body).

## Why sources and branches matter

- The **source** affects **purity, storage, allergies and religious or cultural concerns**. For example, some products of animal origin may matter to some patients.
- The **branches** give you a map of the subject. You will meet each one in later lessons.
