## Why eye products are special

The eye is delicate and has little defence against germs. Therefore **eye drops and eye ointments must be sterile**, and have to be handled carefully.

- Made in sterile conditions, with a **preservative** in multi-dose bottles.
- **Discard 28 days after opening**, or sooner if the label says so. A bottle opened in hospital or pharmacy for one patient is not shared.
- Single-dose **unit droppers** are used once and thrown away, without preservative.
- **Never share** eye drops or ointments, even in the family.
- Do not use if the drops are **cloudy**, discoloured, or past the expiry date.
- Store as the label says. Some are kept in the fridge.

## Common eye conditions

| Condition | What it is | Main points |
|---|---|---|
| Bacterial conjunctivitis | Infection of the membrane covering the eye and lids | Red, sticky eye with yellow or green discharge. Often one eye first, then both |
| Viral conjunctivitis | Viral infection, often with a cold | Watery, red eye, very contagious. Usually settles without antibiotics |
| Allergic conjunctivitis | Reaction to pollen, dust or animals | Itching, watering, redness in both eyes |
| Stye (hordeolum) | Infection of an eyelash gland | A painful lump on the lid. Warm compress |
| Blepharitis | Inflamed lid margins | Crusting, itching. Lid hygiene |
| Trachoma | Chlamydia infection, repeated over years | Can scar the lid and cause blindness. Treated with azithromycin or tetracycline ointment. Prevented by face washing, clean water and sanitation |
| Dry eye | Too little tear film | Gritty feeling. Artificial tears |
| Keratitis | Infection of the cornea | Pain, light sensitivity, reduced vision. **Urgent** |

## Antibacterial eye preparations

| Drug | Notes |
|---|---|
| Chloramphenicol | Drops and ointment. Widely used. A rare risk of serious blood problems means short courses and no repeat use without advice |
| Tetracycline or oxytetracycline | Ointment. Used in trachoma and conjunctivitis |
| Gentamicin, tobramycin | Drops for bacterial infections, including some serious ones |
| Ciprofloxacin, ofloxacin | Drops for corneal ulcers and severe infections |
| Fusidic acid | Viscous drops, used twice daily |
| Neomycin and polymyxin combinations | Used in some countries. Allergy is possible |

**Points**

- Usual treatment lasts **5 to 7 days**, and drops are used **several times a day**, while ointment is used **less often** or at night.
- Eye **ointment** blurs vision for a while, so it is useful at **bedtime**.
- **Drops** are used first in the daytime, and if both drops and ointment are prescribed, drops go in first and ointment last.
- Wash discharge away with **clean, cooled boiled water** and fresh cotton wool, wiping from the inner corner outwards.

## Antiviral and antifungal preparations

- **Aciclovir** eye ointment for **herpes simplex** infection of the eye.
- **Ganciclovir** gel for herpes infection of the cornea, in some settings.
- **Antifungal** eye drops, such as natamycin, are used for fungal keratitis, **under eye specialist care**.
- **Viral conjunctivitis** does not respond to antibiotics. Cold compresses, clean hands, and time are the treatment. It is highly **contagious**.

## Antiallergic eye preparations

- **Antihistamine drops**: azelastine, olopatadine.
- **Mast-cell stabilisers**: sodium cromoglicate. They work best when used **regularly** before the allergy season.
- **Cold compresses** and avoiding the allergen.
- Oral antihistamines may also help.

## Corticosteroid eye drops

Examples: **prednisolone**, **dexamethasone**, **betamethasone**, **hydrocortisone** eye preparations.

They reduce inflammation, for example after eye surgery or in uveitis. However:

- They can **worsen herpes and fungal infections**, causing scarring and blindness.
- Long use **raises eye pressure** (steroid glaucoma) and can cause **cataract**.
- They may **delay healing** of corneal wounds.
- They must be **prescribed and monitored by a doctor**, with eye checks. **Do not supply on request** for a "red eye".

Combination drops containing a steroid and an antibiotic should not be used without a prescription.

## Giving eye drops and ointment

**Drops**

1. **Wash hands.**
2. Tilt the head back and look up.
3. Pull the **lower lid down** to make a pouch.
4. Hold the bottle above the eye without touching it, and let **one drop** fall into the pouch.
5. **Close the eye gently** for 1 to 2 minutes, and press the inner corner (next to the nose) to reduce drainage.
6. **Wait 5 minutes** between different drops. Wipe away extra drops.

**Ointment**

1. Wash hands, tilt the head back and pull the lower lid down.
2. Squeeze a **thin line** (about 1 cm) into the pouch without touching the eye.
3. Close the eye and blink gently. Vision blurs for a few minutes.

**Contact lenses:** do not wear them during an eye infection. Some drops contain preservatives that soften lenses, so remove lenses first unless the label permits.

## Prevention of spread

- **Wash hands** often, and avoid touching or rubbing the eyes.
- **Use your own towel** and pillow.
- Stay **away from work or school** if the eye is red and discharging, as advised.
- **Throw away** eye make-up used during infection.
- Teach **face washing** in children, as it helps to prevent trachoma.

## When to refer urgently

- **Pain** in the eye, or **reduced vision**, or **sensitivity to light**.
- A **red painful eye**, particularly with **headache or halos around lights**.
- **Injury**, a foreign body in the eye, or a **chemical splash**. Rinse with clean water for a long time and go to hospital.
- A **newborn baby** with a sticky eye.
- **Contact lens wearers** with a red eye.
- Symptoms that do **not improve in 48 hours**, or an eye that is **getting worse**.
- A **squint** or an **eye that does not move normally**.
