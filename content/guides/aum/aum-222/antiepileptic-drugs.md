## What is epilepsy?

**Epilepsy** is a long-term condition in which sudden bursts of abnormal electrical activity in the brain cause repeated **seizures**. It is a **medical condition**. It is **not contagious**, and it is not caused by witchcraft or curses. Many people with epilepsy live normal lives with proper treatment. Stigma stops people from seeking care.

| Seizure type | What happens |
|---|---|
| Generalised tonic-clonic | Loss of consciousness, stiffening, then jerking of the whole body |
| Absence | Short "blank" spells, mostly in children |
| Focal (partial) | Twitching, odd feelings or behaviour in one part, with or without loss of awareness |
| Febrile convulsion | Seizure with high fever in young children. Not necessarily epilepsy |

## Antiepileptic drugs

| Drug | Used for | Notes and main side effects |
|---|---|---|
| Carbamazepine | Focal and tonic-clonic seizures | Drowsiness, dizziness, double vision, rash (stop and report). Many interactions |
| Phenytoin | Focal and tonic-clonic seizures | Gum overgrowth, unsteadiness, rash, hairiness. Narrow safety margin |
| Sodium valproate | Most seizure types, including absence | Weight gain, tremor, hair loss, liver effects. **Harmful in pregnancy** |
| Phenobarbital | Tonic-clonic and focal, often where cost is a problem | Drowsiness, slowed thinking, behaviour change in children |
| Lamotrigine | Many seizure types | Serious rash (report any rash at once) |
| Levetiracetam | Many seizure types | Tiredness, irritability, mood changes |
| Ethosuximide | Absence seizures | Nausea, sleepiness |
| Diazepam, lorazepam, midazolam | **Stopping a prolonged seizure** | Drowsiness, slow breathing. Given by injection, rectally or by mouth lining |

### How they work

They **calm the over-excited nerve cells** in the brain, by affecting sodium or calcium channels, or by boosting the calming chemical GABA.

## Principles of treatment

- Treatment aims at **no seizures, with the fewest side effects**, usually with **one drug** at first.
- It is **long term**, often for years.
- **Take every dose on time.** Missed doses can cause seizures.
- **Never stop suddenly.** The doctor reduces the dose slowly.
- **Do not switch brands** or formulations without advice. For some drugs, a change can alter blood levels.
- Some drugs need **blood level tests** or **liver tests**.

## Interactions and cautions

- **Enzyme inducers** (carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital) speed up the breakdown of other drugs, including **oral contraceptives**, some antiretrovirals and antimalarials, and warfarin. **Women should use another method of contraception** or be reviewed by a doctor.
- **Valproate** can raise levels of some other antiepileptics.
- **Alcohol** and sedatives increase drowsiness and can trigger seizures.
- Some drugs can **lower the seizure threshold**, such as tramadol, some antidepressants, antipsychotics and theophylline. Tell the pharmacist about any epilepsy before supplying other medicines.
- **Antacids** may reduce absorption of some antiepileptics. Space the doses as the pharmacist advises.

## Women and pregnancy

- **Valproate** is associated with **birth defects** and **developmental problems** in children. It should **not** be used in girls and women who can become pregnant unless no other drug works and a risk programme is followed. **Do not stop treatment on your own.** Speak to the doctor.
- A woman planning pregnancy should **see the doctor before conceiving**. **Folic acid** is usually advised.
- Never stop the drug when pregnant without medical advice, because seizures also harm mother and baby.

## First aid for a convulsion

1. **Stay calm** and note the **time**.
2. **Clear the space** and protect the head with something soft.
3. **Loosen** tight clothing around the neck.
4. **Do not restrain** the person, and **do not put anything in the mouth**.
5. When the jerking stops, **turn the person on their side** (recovery position) and keep the airway clear.
6. **Stay with them** until fully awake.

**Call for urgent help** if:

- the seizure lasts **more than 5 minutes**, or seizures follow one after another;
- it is the **first seizure**, or the person is **injured, pregnant, or has trouble breathing** afterwards;
- the person is a **small child with fever**, or does not wake up.

## Counselling

- Take the medicine **at the same times every day**, and use a reminder or pill box.
- **Do not stop** or change the dose without the doctor.
- Avoid **alcohol** and **lack of sleep**, which can trigger seizures.
- **Report** rash, fever, unusual bruising, yellow eyes, or marked mood changes.
- Take care with **driving**, **swimming alone**, **heights**, and **cooking over open fire**, as advised by the doctor.
- Carry a **card or note** saying that you have epilepsy and what medicine you take.
- Brush and floss, and visit the dentist, if on **phenytoin**, to look after the gums.
