## What is a tablet?

A **tablet** is a solid dose of medicine made by pressing one or more drugs together with other ingredients (excipients) into a single unit. Tablets are the most widely used dosage form in the world.

### Common types of tablet

| Type | How it is used |
|---|---|
| Plain compressed tablet | Swallowed whole with water |
| Coated tablet | Swallowed whole; the coating improves taste, protection or release |
| Enteric-coated tablet | Swallowed whole; releases the drug in the intestine |
| Chewable tablet | Chewed before swallowing (for example, some antacids) |
| Dispersible tablet | Dissolved or dispersed in water before taking |
| Effervescent tablet | Dissolved in water; fizzes as it dissolves |
| Sublingual tablet | Placed under the tongue and allowed to dissolve |
| Modified-release tablet | Releases the drug slowly over many hours |

## Why tablets are so popular

- The **dose is accurate**, because each tablet is made to the same weight and strength.
- They are **cheap** to produce in large numbers.
- They are **convenient**: light, compact, easy to carry and to take.
- They are **stable**, so they last longer than most liquids.
- They are **easy to identify** by shape, colour and markings.
- They can be **coated** to hide taste or protect the drug, and made to release the drug slowly.

## Disadvantages

- Some drugs cannot be compressed into a tablet.
- Children, elderly patients and unconscious patients may be unable to swallow them.
- A tablet must first break up and dissolve, so the effect is slower than from a liquid.
- Some drugs irritate the stomach.
- The taste can be bitter if the tablet is not coated.

## Tablet excipients

A tablet contains much more than the drug. The extra ingredients are called **excipients**, and each has a job.

| Excipient | What it does | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Diluent (filler) | Adds bulk so the tablet is big enough to handle | Lactose, starch, microcrystalline cellulose |
| Binder | Holds the particles together | Starch paste, gelatin, acacia, polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) |
| Disintegrant | Breaks the tablet apart after swallowing | Starch, sodium starch glycolate |
| Lubricant | Reduces friction so the tablet does not stick in the die | Magnesium stearate, talc |
| Glidant | Helps the powder flow smoothly into the die | Colloidal silica, talc |
| Colours, flavours, sweeteners | Improve appearance and taste | Approved dyes and flavours |

**Lubricant vs glidant:** a lubricant works at the **surface** of the tablet and die, so the tablet comes out cleanly. A glidant works on the **powder**, so it flows evenly. Talc can act as both.

## How a tablet is made

Before compression, the powder is usually made into granules, mixed with the lubricant, and checked. The tablet machine then repeats a cycle of three steps:

1. **Die filling.** The granules flow from the hopper into the die (a hole in the machine). The lower punch sits low in the die so the right volume of granules fills the space.
2. **Tablet formation.** The upper punch comes down and presses the granules against the lower punch with great force. The granules are squeezed into a solid tablet.
3. **Ejection.** The upper punch lifts away, and the lower punch rises and pushes the finished tablet out of the die. A scraper pushes it off the machine, and the cycle starts again.

## Tablet coating

**Coating** means putting a thin layer of material over the outside of the tablet.

### Why tablets are coated

- To hide a bad **taste or smell**
- To protect the drug from **air, moisture and light**
- To protect the **stomach** from a drug that irritates it
- To protect the **drug** from stomach acid
- To control **where or how fast** the drug is released
- To improve **appearance**, make the tablet easier to swallow, and help with identification

### Types of coating

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| Sugar coating | Several layers of sugar syrup. Looks smooth and tastes sweet, but adds a lot of size and weight and takes a long time to apply. |
| Film coating | A thin layer of polymer, applied faster. The tablet stays close to its original size and weight. This is the most common modern coating. |
| Enteric coating | A coating that does not dissolve in stomach acid but dissolves in the small intestine. |

## Packaging and storage

- **Blister packs** and **strips** protect each tablet from moisture and make it easy to count doses.
- **Bottles** (glass or plastic) must be tightly closed. A small bag of drying agent (desiccant) may be placed in the bottle. It should not be swallowed.
- Store in a **cool, dry place**, away from direct sunlight.
- Always check the expiry date and the storage instruction on the label.

## Helping patients take tablets correctly

- Sit or stand upright and take a few sips of water first.
- Place the tablet on the tongue and swallow with a **full glass of water**.
- Do not lie down straight after, as the tablet can get stuck in the food pipe.
- **Do not crush or chew** enteric-coated and modified-release tablets, unless told to.
- Chewable, dispersible and effervescent tablets are the exceptions: follow the instructions on the label.
