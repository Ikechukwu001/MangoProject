## What is extemporaneous compounding?

**Extemporaneous compounding** means preparing a medicine in the pharmacy, **for one named patient**, when the prescription is received. The word comes from the Latin *ex tempore*, "at the time".

It is used when:

- no suitable **ready-made product** exists, for example a liquid for a child who cannot swallow tablets;
- the patient needs a **strength or dose** that is not made commercially;
- the patient is **allergic** to a dye, preservative or other ingredient in the ready-made product;
- a ready-made product is **out of stock**;
- a strong product has to be **diluted**, for example a cream.

It also has limits. Extemporaneous preparations are made by hand and are **not tested** like factory-made medicines, so their **stability is often not known**. For this reason the pharmacist decides whether a preparation should be made, uses a reliable **formula from an approved reference**, and decides the **expiry date**. A pharmacy technician follows the formula exactly, under the pharmacist's supervision, and keeps clean, accurate records.

## The key terms

| Term | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Freshly prepared | Made at the time of supply or very shortly before, and used within a very short time | The product spoils quickly, so it cannot be made in advance |
| Recently prepared | Made a short time before supply and kept for a longer but still limited period, under stated storage conditions | The product keeps for a while, but not for months |
| Potency | How much active drug the preparation contains, compared with the amount it should contain | Too little drug means treatment fails, and too much is unsafe |
| Efficacy | The ability to produce the intended effect | A preparation that does not work is no use, however carefully it was made |
| Shelf life | The time during which the preparation stays good enough to use when stored correctly | It sets the expiry or discard date |

### Freshly prepared and recently prepared

Both terms are about **how soon a preparation must be used after it is made**. A **freshly prepared** product has the shortest life, because it breaks down or grows germs quickly. A **recently prepared** product has a longer life, but it still has a clear limit.

Textbooks and pharmacopoeias give different time limits for the two terms. The idea is the same in all of them: freshly prepared means a very short life, and recently prepared a longer but still limited one. **In an examination, use the limits given in the textbook your school follows.** For a product you are actually dispensing, the pharmacist sets the discard date from the reference used for that formula.

Products that often need a short life include water-based liquids with no preservative, and powders that are mixed with water at the time of dispensing, such as some antibiotic liquids. After mixing, these keep for only a short time, and the label states how long.

### Potency

In dispensing, **potency** means how much active drug a preparation actually contains, usually written as a **percentage of the amount stated on the label**. A preparation can lose potency if the drug breaks down while it is stored. A figure often used in stability work is that a product should keep **at least 90%** of its stated strength until its expiry date.

In pharmacology the word has a second meaning. A drug is **more potent** if a smaller dose gives the same effect. Read the question carefully to see which meaning is intended.

### Efficacy

**Efficacy** is whether the medicine **works**, that is, produces the intended effect. Manufactured medicines are tested for efficacy before they are sold. Extemporaneous preparations are not, so there is **less evidence** that they work. Efficacy can fall if the drug breaks down, if it is not mixed evenly, or if it is made with the wrong ingredients or quantities. This is why a tested formula must be followed exactly.

### Shelf life

**Shelf life** is the period from the time a preparation is made until it **no longer meets its quality standards**: strength, appearance, safety and, for liquids, freedom from germs. This applies only when the preparation is **stored as directed** in a **sealed, undamaged container**. The end of the shelf life is the **expiry date**, or on a pharmacy label, the **discard date**.

## What shortens shelf life?

| Factor | What it does | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| Water | Drugs break down in water and germs grow in it | Keep dry products dry, mix powders with water only when needed, and use a preservative where it is suitable |
| Heat | Speeds up breakdown and germ growth | Store cool, or in a refrigerator if the label says so |
| Light | Breaks down light-sensitive drugs | Use an amber bottle and keep it away from sunlight |
| Air | Oxidises some drugs | Close the container tightly |
| Germs | Spoil the preparation and can harm the patient | Use clean equipment, clean hands, purified water and clean containers |
| Container | Some drugs react with or stick to certain plastics, and clear glass lets light through | Use the container the formula recommends |
| Other ingredients | Some ingredients react with each other | Use a tested formula and never swap ingredients |

## Working out a discard date

Count the days **from the day of preparation as day 0**.

**Example 1.** *Prepared on 5 October. Shelf life 14 days.*

- 5 + 14 = **19 October**

**Example 2: crossing into a new month.** *Prepared on 20 October. Shelf life 14 days.*

1. Days left in October after the 20th: 31 − 20 = 11
2. Days still to count: 14 − 11 = 3
3. The discard date is **3 November**.

If you are unsure how the pharmacy counts, ask the pharmacist and use the same method every time.

## Working out the strength left

**Example.** *A suspension is labelled 250 mg in 5 mL. Its potency has fallen to 90%.*

- Drug in each 5 mL = 250 × 90 ÷ 100 = **225 mg**
- The patient receives 250 − 225 = **25 mg** less per dose than the label says.

## What goes on the label

Every extemporaneous preparation should carry:

- the **name and strength** of the preparation, and the patient's name;
- the **date of preparation**;
- the **discard date**, or a statement such as "Discard after (date)";
- the **storage** instruction, for example "Store in a refrigerator" or "Keep in a cool place";
- "**Shake the bottle well before use**" for suspensions;
- the directions for use, in plain words.

The pharmacist checks the label and the expiry date before the medicine is handed to the patient.
