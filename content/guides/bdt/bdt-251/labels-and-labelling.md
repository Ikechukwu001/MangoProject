## What is a label?

A **label** is the written, printed or graphic information fixed to the container or pack of a medicine. **Labelling** is the wider term: it covers everything written on the container, the outer carton and any leaflet inside.

For the patient, the label is often the only instruction they will read again at home, days after leaving the pharmacy. That makes it one of the most important safety tools in dispensing.

## Functions of a label

A label:

- **identifies** the medicine: name, strength and dosage form;
- **tells the patient how to use it:** how much, how often, for how long and by which route;
- **warns** about precautions and special risks;
- gives **storage** instructions and the **expiry date**;
- gives **legal and traceability information**, such as the manufacturer, batch number and registration number;
- shows **who the medicine is for** and **who supplied it**;
- helps **prevent errors** by letting the patient, the nurse and the pharmacist check the medicine at any time.

## Qualities of an ideal label

An ideal label is:

- **Legible:** easy to read, in clear print or neat handwriting.
- **Accurate:** exactly matches the prescription.
- **Complete:** has all the required information.
- **Clear and simple:** uses plain words the patient understands.
- **Permanent:** the ink does not smudge or fade, and it is not spoiled by moisture.
- **Firmly attached:** it stays on the container for the whole life of the medicine.
- **Well placed:** it does not cover the expiry date or the manufacturer's information.

## Types of label

| Type | Prepared by | What it contains |
|---|---|---|
| Manufacturer's label | The manufacturer | Product name (brand and generic), strength, dosage form, quantity, batch number, manufacturing and expiry dates, storage conditions, manufacturer's name, and the NAFDAC registration number |
| Dispensing label | The pharmacy | Patient's name, date, name and strength of the medicine, directions for use, quantity, and the pharmacy's name and address |
| Auxiliary label | The pharmacy (often pre-printed) | A short warning or piece of advice that adds to the main directions |
| Special instructions | The pharmacy | Extra directions written for this patient, for example a reducing dose schedule |

### The dispensing label

A dispensing label normally includes:

1. Name and address of the pharmacy
2. Date of dispensing
3. Patient's name
4. Name and strength of the medicine
5. Directions for use, in plain words
6. Quantity supplied
7. Any auxiliary label or special instruction needed
8. "Keep out of the reach of children"

### Auxiliary labels

An auxiliary label gives one short, clear message. Common ones are:

| Auxiliary label | Used for | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Shake the bottle well before use | Suspensions, emulsions | The contents separate on standing |
| For external use only | Creams, ointments, lotions, liniments | Prevents swallowing a product made for the skin |
| Take with or after food | Medicines that irritate the stomach, such as ibuprofen and diclofenac | Food protects the stomach |
| Take on an empty stomach | Some antibiotics, such as cloxacillin, and rifampicin | Food reduces how much is absorbed |
| Do not drink alcohol | Metronidazole | Alcohol can cause nausea, vomiting and flushing |
| May cause drowsiness. If affected, do not drive or operate machinery | Chlorphenamine, promethazine and other sedating antihistamines | Drowsiness makes driving and machine work dangerous |
| Complete the full course | Antibiotics | Stopping early lets the infection return |
| Do not chew or crush | Enteric-coated and modified-release tablets | Crushing destroys the coating or releases the dose too quickly |
| Dissolve in water before taking | Dispersible and effervescent tablets | They are made to be taken as a liquid |
| Store in a refrigerator | Insulin before it is opened, and other products that need cold storage | Heat damages the medicine |
| Protect from light | Light-sensitive medicines | Light breaks down the drug |

### Special instructions

Special instructions are extra directions added for one patient when the standard label is not enough. Examples are a reducing dose ("Take 4 tablets daily for 2 days, then 2 tablets daily for 2 days") or "Use only in the left ear". Write them in plain words and check them with the pharmacist.

## Worked example: writing a dispensing label

*Prescription: Amoxicillin 500 mg capsules, 1 capsule tds for 5 days.*

1. Translate the abbreviation: tds means three times a day.
2. Work out the quantity: 1 capsule × 3 times × 5 days = **15 capsules**.
3. Choose the auxiliary label: *Complete the full course.*
4. Write the label:

```
[Pharmacy name and address]
Date: (date of dispensing)
Patient: (full name)

AMOXICILLIN 500 mg CAPSULES
Take ONE capsule THREE times a day for 5 days.
Quantity: 15 capsules

Complete the full course.
Keep out of the reach of children.
```

## Checking and common label errors

Read the label against the prescription **at least three times**: when you take the medicine from the shelf, when you write the label, and before you hand the medicine over.

Common mistakes to watch for:

- the wrong patient's name on the label;
- the wrong strength or dosage form;
- directions that do not match the prescription;
- abbreviations or Latin terms the patient cannot read;
- a label stuck over the expiry date or the manufacturer's information;
- labelling the lid instead of the container, since lids get swapped;
- two similar-looking products labelled at the same time and mixed up.
