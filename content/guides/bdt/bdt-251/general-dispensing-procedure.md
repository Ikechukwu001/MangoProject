## What is dispensing?

**Dispensing** is the whole process that begins when a prescription is received and ends when the medicine is in the patient's hands, with the advice they need to use it safely. It includes interpreting the prescription, selecting or preparing the medicine, labelling it, checking it, recording it and advising the patient.

It is much more than counting tablets. Every step exists to make sure the **right medicine** reaches the **right patient**, in the **right dose**, in the **right form**, with the **right advice**.

A pharmacy technician works **under the supervision of a pharmacist**. The pharmacist is responsible for the final check and for any clinical judgement.

## Steps taken while dispensing

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1. Receive | Greet the patient politely. Confirm the name on the prescription is the patient's. Note any allergy the patient mentions and tell the pharmacist. |
| 2. Check | Read the whole prescription. Is it clear, complete, dated and signed? Check the patient's details, the medicine, strength, dose, route, frequency and duration. See the lesson on Prescriptions. |
| 3. Interpret | Work out what each term and abbreviation means. Calculate the quantity to supply. |
| 4. Select | Take the right medicine from the shelf. Check its name, strength and dosage form, and its expiry date. Use FEFO. |
| 5. Prepare | Count, measure or compound the medicine using clean equipment. |
| 6. Label and package | Write the label, add any auxiliary labels, and put the medicine in a suitable container. |
| 7. Final check | The pharmacist checks the finished medicine, label and container against the original prescription. |
| 8. Record | Enter the supply in the dispensing record. Controlled drugs also go in the controlled drugs register. File the prescription as the pharmacy's rules require. |
| 9. Hand over and counsel | Confirm you are giving the medicine to the right person. Explain how to use it. |
| 10. Tidy up | Return stock to the shelf, clean the counter and equipment. |

### Step 4 in detail: selecting the medicine

- Read the label of the container **when you take it from the shelf**.
- Check the **expiry date**. Never dispense a medicine that is expired or close to expiry unless the pharmacist agrees.
- Use **FEFO** (first expiry, first out). This means stock that expires soonest is dispensed first, so less stock is wasted.
- Look at the medicine itself. Tablets that are cracked, discoloured or sticky, or liquids that look cloudy when they should be clear, should be shown to the pharmacist.

### Step 5 in detail: preparing the medicine

- Wash your hands and keep the work area clean and tidy.
- Use a counting tray and spatula. Do not touch tablets or capsules with your hands.
- Clean the tray between different medicines so no tablets are left behind.
- Measure liquids at eye level, using a clean measure.

## Worked example: how much to supply?

The rule is: **amount per dose × doses per day × number of days**.

**Example 1.** *Paracetamol 500 mg tablets: 2 tablets four times a day for 3 days.*

2 tablets × 4 times × 3 days = **24 tablets**

**Example 2.** *Amoxicillin suspension 250 mg/5 mL: 5 mL three times a day for 5 days.*

5 mL × 3 times × 5 days = **75 mL**

Always check which pack sizes are available, and ask the pharmacist what to do when the amount needed does not match a pack size.

## Addressing patients

How you speak to patients affects whether they trust you and follow your advice.

- **Greet** the patient politely and use a respectful title, such as Sir, Ma, Mr or Mrs.
- **Speak the patient's language** when you can, or use simple words. Avoid medical terms.
- Give the patient your **full attention**: look at them, listen, and do not rush.
- Be **patient and kind** with elderly patients, children and those who are anxious or in pain.
- Protect **privacy**. Keep your voice low and, where possible, counsel in a quiet corner. Never discuss one patient's medicine in front of others.
- Be **honest**. If you do not know an answer, say so and ask the pharmacist.
- Be **culturally respectful** and never judge a patient for their condition or their questions.

### Counselling points

When you hand over a medicine, make sure the patient knows:

1. **What it is for.**
2. **How much** to take and **how often**.
3. **How long** to keep taking it, and to finish a full course of antibiotics.
4. Whether to take it **with food** or **on an empty stomach**.
5. The **common side effects** and what to do if they happen.
6. How to **store** it, and to keep it out of the reach of children.
7. What to do about a **missed dose**.
8. When to **return** or seek help.

Then check understanding: ask the patient to tell you how they will take the medicine. This is called the **teach-back** method.

## Preventing dispensing errors

Most errors can be prevented by good habits:

- Read the label **three times**: at the shelf, when labelling, and before handing over.
- **Never guess.** Unclear writing, doses or names go to the pharmacist.
- Be careful with **look-alike, sound-alike** medicines. For example, hydralazine and hydroxyzine, or metformin and metronidazole. Shelve them apart and read the full name every time.
- Work in a **tidy, quiet** area. Avoid interruptions during the final count and check.
- Have every prescription **checked by a second person**.
- Use the patient's full name when handing over, so medicines do not go to the wrong person.
- **Record** every error or near miss, so the pharmacy can learn from it.
