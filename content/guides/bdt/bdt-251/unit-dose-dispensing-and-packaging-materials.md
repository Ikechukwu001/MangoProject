## The unit dose dispensing system

A **unit dose dispensing system** supplies each dose of a medicine as its own **separate, labelled package**, ready to be given to the patient. The curriculum calls it **UDSS**.

It is mainly used in hospitals for **in-patients**. The pharmacy prepares the doses and sends them to the ward, usually in a labelled tray or cassette for each patient. The nurse takes the dose from the package and gives it, with no counting, measuring or pouring on the ward.

Each unit dose package is labelled with the **medicine's name and strength**, and usually the **batch number and expiry date**, so the medicine can be identified right up to the moment it is given.

### How it compares with other ways of supplying wards

| System | How it works | Main problem |
|---|---|---|
| Ward stock | Bulk containers of common medicines are kept on the ward | Easy to run short, easy to waste, and errors are more likely |
| Individual patient supply | A named container is supplied for each patient | Can leave a lot of leftover medicine after discharge |
| Unit dose | Pre-packed, labelled single doses for each patient | Needs more pharmacy staff, equipment and time |

### Importance and advantages

- **Fewer medication errors.** Each dose is checked in the pharmacy, so a wrong dose or wrong medicine is less likely to reach the patient.
- **Less waste.** Only the doses that are needed are supplied. If a patient is discharged or the prescription changes, unopened doses can be returned and reused.
- **Better stock control** and accountability, because the pharmacy knows exactly what was supplied.
- **Saves nurses' time**, so they can spend more of it caring for patients.
- **Safer for infection control.** The medicine is not handled again on the ward.
- **Easier to charge accurately** for what the patient actually used.
- **Helps patients take their medicines correctly**, since each dose is ready to give.

### Disadvantages

- It needs **extra pharmacy staff, equipment and space**.
- It takes **more time and money** to set up and run.
- Repacking a medicine into a new container can change how long it stays stable. Pharmacies set a **shorter expiry date** for repacked doses, so the packing material and expiry must be chosen carefully.
- It needs good records and clear organisation to work well.

## Packaging materials

The container must protect the medicine until the patient has used it. The packaging that is **in direct contact** with the medicine is the **primary packaging**, such as a bottle, blister, tube or ampoule. The **outer carton** is **secondary packaging**.

### Qualities of good packaging

Good packaging:

- **protects** the medicine from light, moisture, air, heat and germs;
- does **not react** with the medicine, absorb it or release harmful substances into it;
- is **non-toxic**;
- **holds the label** firmly;
- is **easy and safe to use**, and can show if it has been tampered with;
- is **affordable**.

### The materials

| Material | Used for | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Glass (clear or amber) | Liquids, injections, some tablets | Does not react with most medicines, see-through, can be sealed well. Amber glass protects against light. | Heavy and breakable |
| Plastic (polyethylene, polypropylene, PET, PVC) | Bottles, jars, blisters, droppers, squeeze bottles | Light, unbreakable, cheap, can be moulded into many shapes | Some let in moisture or air, and some can absorb or react with certain medicines |
| Metal (aluminium) | Collapsible tubes, foil strips and blisters, aerosol cans | Strong barrier to light, moisture and air | Can react with some products unless it is lined |
| Paper and board | Cartons, powder papers, sachets, leaflets | Cheap, easy to print on | Gives little protection against moisture unless waxed or coated |
| Rubber | Stoppers for vials, dropper teats | Flexible, seals well | Can absorb some medicines or release substances into them |

## Matching the pack to the preparation

| Preparation | Suitable packaging |
|---|---|
| Tablets and capsules | Blister packs, foil strips, or plastic or glass bottles. Moisture-sensitive products may include a **desiccant** (a drying agent in a small bag, which must not be swallowed). |
| Powders | Wide-mouth jars or tubs, or waxed papers and sachets for divided powders |
| Dusting powders | Containers with a sifter top |
| Oral liquids | Glass or plastic bottles with a tight cap. Use **amber** bottles for light-sensitive products. A measuring cup or spoon is often included. |
| Eye, ear and nasal drops | Small plastic or glass dropper bottles. Eye drops are sterile and must be used within the time on the label after opening. |
| Ointments and creams | Collapsible aluminium or plastic tubes, or wide-mouth jars |
| Suppositories and pessaries | Foil or plastic strips |
| Injections | Glass **ampoules** (sealed single-dose containers) or **vials** with rubber stoppers |
| Aerosols and inhalers | Pressurised metal cans fitted with a valve |

### Closures

- **Child-resistant closures** reduce the risk of children swallowing medicines. Use them unless the patient is unable to open them and asks for an easy-open cap.
- **Tamper-evident seals** show whether a pack has been opened.
- **Tight, well-fitting caps** keep out air and moisture.
