## What is a prescription?

A **prescription** is a written order from an **authorised prescriber** (such as a doctor or dentist) to a pharmacist, asking for a medicine to be supplied to a named patient. It is also a **legal document**. It must be kept and treated carefully.

The pharmacy technician's job is to understand it correctly. The pharmacist is responsible for checking it.

## Parts of a prescription

| Part | What it contains |
|---|---|
| Prescriber details | Name, qualification, address and contact of the doctor or clinic |
| Patient details | Name, age, sex, address, and weight for children |
| Date | When the prescription was written |
| Superscription | The symbol **Rx**, from the Latin *recipe*, meaning "take" |
| Inscription | The main part: the name and strength of the drug, the dosage form and the quantity |
| Subscription | Instructions to the dispenser, for example how to prepare or how much to supply |
| Signa | Directions for the patient, which are copied onto the label. Often written "Sig." or "S." |
| Signature | The prescriber's signature, and a stamp where used |

### A sample prescription

```
City Medical Centre
Dr A. Okafor, MBBS

Date: 12/03/2026
Patient: Mr Emeka Obi     Age: 34     Sex: M

Rx
    Capsule Amoxicillin 500 mg
    Sig: ONE capsule three times a day for 5 days
    Dispense: 15 capsules

Signed: ______________
```

In this sample, **Rx** is the superscription, the drug name and strength are the inscription, "Dispense: 15 capsules" is the subscription, and the **Sig** line is the signa.

## Qualities of an ideal prescription

An ideal prescription is:

- **legible**: written clearly, in ink, so no letter can be misread;
- **complete**: it has the patient's details, the date, the drug name, strength, dosage form, quantity and directions;
- **clear**: the directions are simple and have only one meaning;
- **signed** by the prescriber, with details that allow the prescriber to be contacted;
- **dated**, because many prescriptions are valid only for a limited time;
- written with the **generic name** of the drug where possible, which reduces confusion between brands;
- free from **doubtful abbreviations**.

## Types of prescription

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| Outpatient (private) | Written on a prescription form or pad and taken by the patient to a pharmacy |
| Hospital (in-patient) | Written on the patient's chart or a ward order form. The pharmacy supplies the ward |
| Repeat | Can be supplied more than once, as the prescriber allows. Used for long-term conditions |
| Controlled drug | For drugs with a risk of abuse or dependence. Has stricter requirements and each supply is entered in a controlled drugs register |
| Emergency or verbal | Given by phone or in person when written is not possible. The pharmacist decides whether to accept it and follows pharmacy policy to get written confirmation |

Some hospitals use computer-printed or electronic prescriptions. They must contain the same information.

## When something is wrong

Do **not** guess and do **not** change a prescription yourself. Refer the prescription to the pharmacist if:

- the handwriting cannot be read with certainty;
- the dose looks too high or too low;
- the patient's details, date or signature are missing;
- the drug or strength does not exist as written;
- the prescription looks altered or suspicious.

The pharmacist can contact the prescriber to confirm or correct it.
