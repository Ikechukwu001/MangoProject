## Ratio

A **ratio** compares two quantities of the **same kind**. We write it with a colon: 1 : 4 is read "one to four".

Pay attention to the wording:

- **1 : 4** means 1 part **to** 4 parts. The total is **5 parts**.
- **1 in 4** means 1 part **in a total of** 4 parts. The other part is 3.

### Simplifying a ratio

Divide both sides by the same number, just as you would with a fraction.

- 250 : 1000 = 1 : 4 (divide both by 250)
- 15 : 45 : 60 = 1 : 3 : 4 (divide all by 15)

Both quantities must be in the **same unit** first. To compare 250 mg with 1 g, change the grams to milligrams: 250 mg : 1000 mg = 1 : 4.

### Dividing a quantity in a ratio

1. Add up the parts.
2. Divide the total by the number of parts to find one part.
3. Multiply by each share.

**Example:** share 120 g in the ratio 1 : 2 : 3.

- Parts: 1 + 2 + 3 = 6
- One part: 120 ÷ 6 = 20 g
- Shares: 1 × 20 = **20 g**, 2 × 20 = **40 g**, 3 × 20 = **60 g**
- Check: 20 + 40 + 60 = 120 g ✓

## Proportion

A **proportion** says that two ratios are equal. If a/b = c/d, then cross-multiplying gives **a × d = b × c**.

There is a quick way to set one up. Write the known pair, then the unknown pair, so that the **same kind of quantity is in the same position**:

```
drug in 5 mL   =   drug in 12 mL
     250 mg              x
```

**Example 1.** *5 mL of a syrup contains 250 mg. How much is in 12 mL?*

- 250 mg is to 5 mL as x is to 12 mL
- x = 250 × 12 ÷ 5 = 3 000 ÷ 5 = **600 mg**

**Example 2.** *20 tablets weigh 10 g. What do 150 tablets weigh?*

- x = 10 × 150 ÷ 20 = 1 500 ÷ 20 = **75 g**

**Example 3.** *A mixture contains 125 mg in each 5 mL. How many millilitres contain 375 mg?*

- x = 375 × 5 ÷ 125 = 1 875 ÷ 125 = **15 mL**

**Example 4.** *A formula uses 3 g of drug for 30 mL. How much is needed for 150 mL?*

- x = 3 × 150 ÷ 30 = **15 g**

### The unitary method

Another way is to find the amount for **one unit**, then multiply. In Example 1: 5 mL holds 250 mg, so 1 mL holds 250 ÷ 5 = 50 mg, and 12 mL holds 12 × 50 = 600 mg. You get the same answer.

## Variation

Variation describes how one quantity changes when another one changes.

### Direct variation

Two quantities vary **directly** when they rise and fall together in the same ratio. If one doubles, the other doubles.

**Example:** a dose is 5 mg for every kilogram of body weight.

| Weight | Dose |
|---|---|
| 12 kg | 5 × 12 = 60 mg |
| 18 kg | 5 × 18 = 90 mg |
| 24 kg | 5 × 24 = 120 mg |

The dose divided by the weight is always 5. That fixed value is the **constant of variation**.

### Inverse variation

Two quantities vary **inversely** when one rises as the other falls. If one doubles, the other halves. Their **product** stays the same.

**Example 1: concentration and volume.** The same amount of drug is dissolved in more and more liquid.

- 20 g in 200 mL gives 10%.
- 20 g in 400 mL gives 5%.
- Check the product: 10 × 200 = 2 000 and 5 × 400 = 2 000.

So a quick rule is: **new strength = old strength × old volume ÷ new volume.**

**Example 2: work and time.** A batch takes 6 hours using 2 machines. How long with 3 machines?

- More machines means less time, so this is inverse.
- 6 × 2 ÷ 3 = **4 hours**

### Telling them apart

Ask: *if one quantity doubles, what happens to the other?*

| Doubles, so the other... | Type | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Doubles | Direct | Dose and body weight |
| Halves | Inverse | Strength and total volume |
