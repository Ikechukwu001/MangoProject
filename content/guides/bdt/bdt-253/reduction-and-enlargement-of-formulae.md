## What is a formula?

A **formula** is a list of the ingredients in a preparation, with the quantity of each, for a stated total amount. It may be written in a book, a pharmacopoeia or on a prescription.

You will often need a different amount from the one the formula gives. A formula for 100 mL might have to be **enlarged** to 5 litres for a hospital, or **reduced** to 30 g to make a small supply.

## The scale factor method

```
scale factor = quantity required ÷ quantity in the formula
```

Then **multiply every ingredient by the scale factor**.

- A factor **greater than 1** enlarges the formula.
- A factor **less than 1** reduces it.

**Example 1: enlargement.** *A formula for 100 mL contains drug A 5 g, drug B 2 g and glycerin 10 mL, with purified water to 100 mL. Make 250 mL.*

- Scale factor = 250 ÷ 100 = **2.5**

| Ingredient | In the formula | × 2.5 |
|---|---|---|
| Drug A | 5 g | **12.5 g** |
| Drug B | 2 g | **5 g** |
| Glycerin | 10 mL | **25 mL** |
| Purified water | to 100 mL | **to 250 mL** |

**Example 2: reduction.** *An ointment formula for 500 g contains A 50 g, B 150 g and C 300 g. Make 60 g.*

- Scale factor = 60 ÷ 500 = **0.12**

| Ingredient | In the formula | × 0.12 |
|---|---|---|
| A | 50 g | **6 g** |
| B | 150 g | **18 g** |
| C | 300 g | **36 g** |
| Total | 500 g | **60 g** ✓ |

The amounts add up to 60 g, so the working is correct.

**Example 3: a very large enlargement.** *A formula for 100 mL is enlarged to make 5 litres (5 000 mL).*

- Scale factor = 5 000 ÷ 100 = **50**
- Drug A: 5 g × 50 = **250 g**

**Example 4: reducing to a small pack.** *A formula for 1 kg contains A 25 g, B 75 g and C 900 g. Make a 30 g tube.*

- 1 kg = 1 000 g, so scale factor = 30 ÷ 1 000 = **0.03**
- A: 25 × 0.03 = **0.75 g**
- B: 75 × 0.03 = **2.25 g**
- C: 900 × 0.03 = **27 g**
- Check: 0.75 + 2.25 + 27 = 30 g ✓

After reducing a formula, some amounts may be too small to weigh accurately. In that case use the aliquot method or a dilution.

## Ingredients in parts

Some formulae give the ingredients as **parts**, in a ratio.

**Example.** *A base is made from three ingredients in the ratio 1 : 2 : 7. Make 500 g.*

1. Total parts = 1 + 2 + 7 = 10
2. One part = 500 ÷ 10 = 50 g
3. The ingredients are **50 g**, **100 g** and **350 g**.
4. Check: 50 + 100 + 350 = 500 g ✓

## Formulae written per dose

When a formula is written for each dose (for example "each 5 mL contains"), find the **number of doses** first.

**Example 1.** *Each 5 mL contains 125 mg of drug. Make 200 mL.*

1. Number of doses = 200 ÷ 5 = 40
2. Drug needed = 40 × 125 mg = 5 000 mg = **5 g**

**Example 2.** *Each 10 mL contains drug A 200 mg and drug B 50 mg. Make 150 mL.*

1. Number of doses = 150 ÷ 10 = 15
2. Drug A = 15 × 200 = 3 000 mg = **3 g**
3. Drug B = 15 × 50 = 750 mg = **0.75 g**

## "Quantity sufficient" and making up to volume

Formulae often end with a line such as *purified water to 100 mL* (written *aqua ad 100 mL* or *q.s. ad*).

This means: dissolve the ingredients, then add water **until the total volume reaches 100 mL**. The dissolved ingredients take up some space, so the water you add will be **less** than 100 mL.

## Checking a scaled formula

- Did you multiply **every** ingredient by the same factor?
- Are all quantities in the **same units** (g with g, mL with mL)?
- Do the weights of solids **add up** to the total weight?
- Is the answer the **right size**? A factor of 2.5 should give amounts a little more than double.
- Are any amounts **too small to weigh** accurately?
