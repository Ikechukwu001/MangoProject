## Why accuracy matters

Every balance and every measure can be slightly wrong. This unavoidable error matters a great deal when the quantity being measured is small, because the error is a bigger share of what you are trying to measure.

## Percentage error

```
percentage error = (error ÷ quantity measured) × 100
```

**Example 1: weighing.** A balance may be wrong by up to **5 mg**.

| Quantity weighed | Percentage error |
|---|---|
| 50 mg | 5 ÷ 50 × 100 = **10%** |
| 500 mg | 5 ÷ 500 × 100 = **1%** |

The same balance is ten times worse at 50 mg than at 500 mg.

**Example 2: measuring a liquid.** You need to measure **10 mL**.

| Measure used | Possible error | Percentage error |
|---|---|---|
| 100 mL cylinder | ± 1 mL | 1 ÷ 10 × 100 = **10%** |
| 10 mL measure | ± 0.2 mL | 0.2 ÷ 10 × 100 = **2%** |

### Choosing a measure

- Use the **smallest measure** that can hold the volume, because it has the smallest error.
- As a rule of thumb, the volume you measure should be **at least 20% (one fifth)** of the capacity of the measure. To measure 10 mL, do not use anything larger than a 50 mL measure.
- Read the bottom of the curve (the meniscus) with your eye level with the liquid.

## Minimum weighable quantity

The **minimum weighable quantity** is the smallest amount that can be weighed on a balance without exceeding the error you are willing to accept.

```
minimum quantity = balance error ÷ (acceptable error as a fraction)
```

**Example 1.** A balance may be wrong by ±5 mg. You accept an error of 5% (0.05).

- Minimum quantity = 5 ÷ 0.05 = **100 mg**

**Example 2.** A balance may be wrong by ±2 mg. You accept an error of 2% (0.02).

- Minimum quantity = 2 ÷ 0.02 = **100 mg**

If the amount you need is **less** than this, weighing it directly gives too much error. You use the aliquot method.

## The aliquot method

An **aliquot** is a measured fraction of a larger quantity. The idea is to weigh a larger amount that the balance can handle accurately, dissolve it in a measured volume, and then measure out just the part that holds the quantity you need.

### For solids

1. Weigh **at least the minimum weighable quantity** of the drug.
2. Dissolve it in a suitable liquid and make it up to a **measured volume**.
3. Work out the **strength** (mg per mL).
4. Measure the aliquot that contains the amount you need.
5. Use the aliquot in the preparation, and discard the rest.

**Example 1.** *You need 15 mg of a drug. The minimum weighable quantity is 100 mg.*

1. Weigh 120 mg of the drug. This is more than 100 mg, so the error is acceptable.
2. Dissolve it in water and make up to 40 mL.
3. Strength = 120 ÷ 40 = 3 mg per mL.
4. Volume needed = 15 ÷ 3 = **5 mL**. Measure 5 mL, which contains 15 mg.

You could also weigh 100 mg and make it up to 50 mL (2 mg per mL), then measure 15 ÷ 2 = 7.5 mL. Both routes give the same amount of drug. Choose numbers that make the aliquot easy to measure.

**Example 2.** *A prescription needs 12 mg of drug in 100 mL of mixture. The minimum weighable quantity is 100 mg.*

1. Weigh 120 mg and dissolve it in water to make 100 mL of stock solution.
2. Strength = 120 ÷ 100 = 1.2 mg per mL.
3. Volume needed = 12 ÷ 1.2 = **10 mL**.
4. Measure 10 mL of the stock solution and add it to the mixture. It is **part of the final 100 mL**, so account for it when you make up to volume.

### For liquids

The same idea works for a small volume of liquid that is too little to measure accurately.

**Example.** *You need 0.2 mL of a liquid.*

1. Measure 1 mL of the liquid and dilute it with a suitable diluent to 10 mL. Now 1 mL of the dilution contains 0.1 mL of the liquid.
2. You need 0.2 mL, so measure **2 mL** of the dilution.

### Good practice

- Use a **diluent that is compatible** with the drug and the final preparation.
- Make up to a **measured volume** accurately, so the strength is known.
- Mix well before taking the aliquot.
- **Discard** the unused stock solution unless the pharmacist says otherwise.
- Keep the working written down so it can be checked.

For solid powders, the same principle is used with a powder diluent. That method, called trituration, is covered in the lesson on dilution and concentration.
