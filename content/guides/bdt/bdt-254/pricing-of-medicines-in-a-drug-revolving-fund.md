## Why pricing decides whether the fund survives

A revolving fund lives or dies on its prices. The money from sales must be enough to **buy the same stock again**, plus cover whatever else the community has decided the fund must pay for. If prices are too low, the fund shrinks. If they are too high, patients cannot afford medicines.

## Replacement cost and historical cost

| | Historical cost | Replacement cost |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | What the fund **paid** for the stock it has now | What it would cost **today** to buy the same stock again |
| Effect when prices rise | Money from sales buys less than before | Money from sales buys the same amount |
| Used by a revolving fund? | No | **Yes** |

### Worked example

*A pack was bought at ₦40. The supplier now charges ₦50.*

- Priced at historical cost, the fund sells at ₦40 (or a little more), then has to find ₦50 for the next pack. It is ₦10 short each time.
- Priced at replacement cost, it sells at ₦50 or more, and the next pack can be bought.

Always check **current supplier prices** before pricing, and review prices regularly, especially when inflation is high or the exchange rate moves.

## What a price must cover

| Part of the price | Reason |
|---|---|
| Replacement cost | To buy the same stock again |
| Allowance for losses | Expiry, damage, theft and spillage mean not all stock is sold |
| Share of running costs | Transport, storage, wages and stationery, at the "full plus other costs" level |
| Subsidy for exemptions | If some patients are exempt, someone must pay for them |
| Rounding | Prices are rounded to amounts that are easy to pay and give change for |

## Markup and margin

Both describe profit, but on **different bases**:

```
profit = selling price − cost
markup (%) = profit ÷ cost × 100
margin (%) = profit ÷ selling price × 100
```

### Worked example

*An item costs ₦80 and sells for ₦100.*

- Profit = 100 − 80 = ₦20
- Markup = 20 ÷ 80 × 100 = **25%**
- Margin = 20 ÷ 100 × 100 = **20%**

The same profit gives two different percentages. Always check which one a question or a price list means.

### Finding the selling price from a markup

*An item costs ₦200 and the markup is 25%.*

- Profit = 200 × 25 ÷ 100 = ₦50
- Selling price = 200 + 50 = **₦250**

Equivalent shortcut: selling price = cost × 1.25 = ₦250.

## Allowing for losses

Not everything the fund buys will be sold. Some stock expires, breaks or goes missing. The sales of the remaining stock must pay for **all** of it.

```
price = replacement cost ÷ (1 − loss fraction)
```

### Worked example

*The replacement cost is ₦50 per item, and 20% of stock is expected to be lost.*

- Loss fraction = 20 ÷ 100 = 0.2
- Price = 50 ÷ (1 − 0.2) = 50 ÷ 0.8 = **₦62.50**

**Check.** *Buy 100 items at ₦50 = ₦5 000. If 20% are lost, only 80 are sold.* 80 × ₦62.50 = **₦5 000**. The fund recovers exactly what it spent.

Notice that adding 20% to the cost (₦60) would **not** be enough: 80 × ₦60 = ₦4 800, which is ₦200 short. The loss is 20% of the stock **sold**, not of the cost, so we divide, not multiply.

The better way to deal with losses is to **prevent them**, using FEFO, stock cards, correct storage and sensible ordering. Allowances should be small and honest, not a way to hide poor management.

## Steps in pricing a medicine

1. **Find the current replacement cost** from the supplier.
2. **Add the allowance for losses**, using the division method above.
3. **Add the share of running costs**, if the fund aims for full plus other costs recovery.
4. **Add any subsidy** needed for exempt patients.
5. **Round** to a convenient amount.
6. **Compare** with what patients can pay, and agree the prices with the committee.
7. **Review** the prices regularly.

### Worked example

*Replacement cost ₦50. Loss allowance 20%. Running costs add ₦5 per item.*

1. Price covering losses = 50 ÷ 0.8 = ₦62.50
2. Add running costs = 62.50 + 5 = ₦67.50
3. Round up to the next ₦5 = **₦70**

The running cost per item is found by dividing the monthly running costs by the number of items expected to be sold. For example, ₦20 000 of running costs and 4 000 items sold gives ₦5 per item.

## Ways of charging patients

| Method | How it works | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fee per item | Each medicine has its own price | Fair, and easy to link to cost | Patients may buy fewer medicines than they need, and prices must be kept up to date |
| Fee per treatment | One price for each type of illness or course of treatment | Simple, and encourages complete courses | Cheap illnesses may pay for costly ones |
| Fee per visit | One fixed fee for any consultation and medicines | Easiest to run | Cost of medicines varies, so the fund can lose |
| Registration or card fee | A fee to register or hold a card | Brings in money in advance | Does not link to the medicines used |

Many schemes mix these, for example a registration fee plus a charge per item.

## Exemptions

Some people, such as young children, the elderly and the very poor, may not be able to pay. A fund must decide:

- **who** is exempt, and how the exemption is checked;
- **how much** it is expected to cost;
- **who** will cover the cost: a government grant, a community fund or higher prices for those who can pay.

### Worked example

*A fund expects to supply medicines worth ₦500 000 at full price. 20% of patients are exempt.*

- Value given to exempt patients = 500 000 × 20 ÷ 100 = **₦100 000**
- Without a subsidy, the fund collects only 500 000 − 100 000 = ₦400 000, and loses ₦100 000 of its capital.

If the other patients must cover this, prices for them must rise by 100 000 ÷ 400 000 × 100 = **25%**.

This is why exemptions should be planned and paid for. If they are not, they are a major reason why funds fail.
