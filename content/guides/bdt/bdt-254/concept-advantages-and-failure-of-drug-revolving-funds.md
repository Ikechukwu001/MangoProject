## What is a drug revolving fund?

A **drug revolving fund (DRF)**, also called a **medicines revolving fund**, is a scheme that assumes that **the cost of common medicines will be recovered** from the people who use them, **put in a separate fund**, and **ploughed back** to buy more medicines to keep the system going.

The fund is started with **seed capital**, which is the money used to buy the first stock. The seed capital may come from the government, a donor, or the community. After that, the money paid for medicines is meant to pay for the next purchase, so the fund **keeps revolving**.

Revolving funds became widespread in Africa after the **Bamako Initiative** of 1987, which encouraged communities to share in the cost of primary health care. Nigerian states and health programmes have also used them to keep common medicines in primary health care centres.

The aim of a DRF is **to keep essential medicines available to the community**. It is not to make a profit. A DRF is unnecessary where medicines are already supplied free and reliably.

## The cycle

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1. Seed capital | Money is provided to buy the first stock |
| 2. Buy | The first stock of essential medicines is bought |
| 3. Store | Stock is stored and recorded on stock cards |
| 4. Sell | Medicines are sold to patients at the agreed prices |
| 5. Collect | Cash is received and a receipt is issued for each sale |
| 6. Bank | The takings are paid into the fund's own bank account |
| 7. Re-order | The money is used to buy the next stock, and the cycle repeats |

The cycle only works if, at step 7, the money in the bank can buy **as much stock as was sold**. Everything about a DRF, including pricing, record keeping and security, protects that one point.

## Advantages

| Advantage | Why |
|---|---|
| Theoretically self-accounting | Once started, the fund is meant to pay for itself without new outside money |
| Encourages community participation and self-reliance | People help to pay for, and often help to manage, their own medicines |
| An incentive for rational use of common medicines | When people pay, they tend to use medicines more carefully and do not waste them |
| Promotes accountability in medicines management | Separate records and a separate bank account show where money and stock went |
| Makes common medicines more available | Regular restocking means fewer stock-outs |

Other benefits often given are that the money collected can **add to central government funds**, that decisions are made **locally**, and that patients may **value** medicines they have paid for and so take them properly.

### Limits and criticisms

A DRF is not a perfect answer, and you should know the arguments against it:

- Fees can **stop poor patients** from getting medicines. This is why exemptions are needed.
- Collecting and recording small payments **costs money and time**.
- Actual collections are often **much lower than expected**.
- It needs **business skills** that many health workers have not been trained in.

## Why drug revolving funds fail

A fund **fails** when it **loses capital**, so that over time it can buy less and less stock until it runs out. These are the causes listed in the curriculum:

| Cause of failure | What happens | How to prevent it |
|---|---|---|
| Seed capital underestimated | There is not enough money to stock all the medicines needed | Work out the cost of a full stock carefully before starting |
| Prices below replacement cost | Sales do not bring back enough money to re-stock | Price at replacement cost, plus an allowance for losses |
| Payment not collected, with no subsidy | Children, the elderly and disabled people are exempt, but nobody pays for them | Decide who is exempt, estimate the cost, and arrange a subsidy to cover it |
| Delays in cash flow | Money from sales is not available when it is time to buy | Bank takings quickly and keep the fund's money where it can be reached |
| Rapid expansion | More medicines or more health units are added without more capital | Expand in step with the money available |
| Theft and loss | Medicines or money are stolen, or medicines expire or spoil | Control stock and cash, use FEFO, and store correctly |
| Inflation outstrips expectations | Medicines cost more each time they are bought | Review prices often and base them on replacement cost |
| Lack of business orientation | Money is tied up in medicines that are rarely used | Stock only what is needed, and review stock regularly |
| Poor financial planning and management | Records are missing and nobody knows the true position | Keep accurate records and report regularly |

## Worked examples

### How inflation shrinks a fund

*A fund sells its whole stock at the price it paid, with no mark-up and no allowance for losses, and receives ₦100 000. The price of the same medicines has risen by 25%.*

1. Cost to re-buy the same stock = 100 000 × 1.25 = ₦125 000
2. Money available = ₦100 000
3. Part of the original stock that can be bought = 100 000 ÷ 125 000 = 0.8, or **80%**

If prices rise by another 25% before the next cycle, the fund buys 80% × 80% = **64%** of the original quantity. The fund shrinks every cycle, even though nobody has stolen anything. This is why prices must be set on **replacement cost**.

### The cost of exemptions

*A health centre expects 1 000 patients to pay ₦500 each. 20% are exempt.*

1. Expected collections = 1 000 × 500 = ₦500 000
2. Exempt patients = 1 000 × 20 ÷ 100 = 200
3. Paying patients = 1 000 − 200 = 800
4. Actual collections = 800 × 500 = **₦400 000**
5. Shortfall = 500 000 − 400 000 = **₦100 000**

The fund can only keep its capital if someone pays this ₦100 000, such as a government grant, a community subsidy, or slightly higher prices for those who can pay.

All prices and amounts in these lessons are made up to practise the arithmetic.
