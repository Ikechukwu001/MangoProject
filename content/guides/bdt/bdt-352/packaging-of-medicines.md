## Functions of packaging

| Function | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Protection | From light, moisture, air, heat, germs, damage and spillage |
| Containment | Holds the medicine in the correct amount |
| Identification | Name, strength, batch number and expiry date |
| Information | Directions, warnings and storage on the label and leaflet |
| Convenience | Easy to open, handle, measure and carry |
| Safety | Child-resistant and tamper-evident features |
| Compliance | Unit-dose and blister packs help patients take their medicines correctly |

The lesson on unit-dose dispensing and packaging materials in BDT 251 covers packaging at the dispensing level. This lesson looks at it from the **manufacturing** side.

## Levels of packaging

| Level | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | In direct contact with the medicine | Bottle, blister, strip, ampoule, vial, tube, sachet |
| Secondary | Holds and presents the primary pack | Carton, box, leaflet |
| Tertiary | For storage and transport | Shipping cartons, pallets, shrink wrap |

## Materials

| Material | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Glass | Clear and inert, airtight, can be sterilised, does not let in water. **Amber glass** blocks light | Heavy and breaks |
| Plastics (such as polyethylene, polypropylene, PVC, PET) | Light, unbreakable, cheap, many shapes | Some let in water vapour or air, can **react with drugs**, soften with heat, and may absorb ingredients |
| Metals (aluminium, tin) | Strong, airtight, light-proof. Used for tubes, cans and foil | Can react with some products, so tubes are lined |
| Paper and board | Cheap, easy to print on. Used for cartons and leaflets | Not protective against moisture on their own |
| Rubber | Used for **stoppers** in vials and droppers | May react with or absorb ingredients |

**Blister packs** combine a plastic or aluminium formed pocket with an aluminium foil lid. They protect each dose, help identify tablets, and support adherence.

## Closures

- **Screw caps**, with or without liners, for bottles.
- **Child-resistant closures**, which need two actions to open, to prevent poisoning.
- **Tamper-evident closures**, which show if the pack has been opened, such as foil seals or break-away rings.
- **Droppers and pumps**, for drops and sprays.
- **Rubber stoppers and crimped caps** for vials.

## Choosing packaging

Ask:

1. Is the container **compatible** with the medicine, so that nothing reacts, leaks or is absorbed?
2. Does it **protect** against the **light, moisture, air and germs** that the medicine needs protection from?
3. Is it **safe**, with a child-resistant or tamper-evident closure where needed?
4. Is it **convenient** for the patient to open, measure and use?
5. Is it **affordable** and available?
6. Is there room for the **full label** and leaflet?

| Product | Suitable pack |
|---|---|
| Light-sensitive liquid | Amber glass bottle with a tight closure |
| Tablets that absorb moisture | Tight container with a desiccant, or a foil blister |
| Cream | Lined metal tube or plastic tube |
| Vaccine | Glass vial with a rubber stopper, kept in the cold chain |
| Eye drops | Dropper bottle with a tamper-evident seal |
| Syrup | Glass or plastic bottle, with a measure |

## Labels and leaflets

The label and leaflet are part of the pack. They carry the **name, strength, form, quantity, batch number, expiry date, storage, directions and warnings**. They must be **clear, accurate and durable**.

## Calculating packs

**Example.** *10 000 tablets, in blisters of 10, with 10 blisters in each carton.*

1. Tablets per carton = 10 × 10 = 100
2. Cartons = 10 000 ÷ 100 = **100 cartons**

**Example.** *A 10-litre batch is filled in 100 mL bottles.*

- Bottles = 10 000 ÷ 100 = **100 bottles**
