## Stability

**Stability** is the ability of a medicine to **keep its quality** (strength, appearance, safety and performance) from the time it is made until it is used. A medicine that is not stable may be **weak**, **harmful** or **spoiled**.

## Types of instability

| Type | What happens | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Chemical | The drug breaks down, so strength falls and harmful products may form | **Hydrolysis** (breakdown by water, as in many antibiotics) and **oxidation** (breakdown by oxygen, as in vitamin C) |
| Physical | The appearance or form changes | Tablets crumble or soften, creams separate, suspensions cake, emulsions crack, colour fades |
| Microbiological | Germs grow | Mould in syrups, creams and mixtures with weak preservatives |

## Factors affecting stability

| Factor | Effect | Control |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Heat speeds up breakdown and may melt or soften products. Cold can freeze and break some | Store at the stated temperature |
| Moisture and humidity | Make powders cake, tablets soften and drugs break down | Keep dry, close tightly, use desiccant or foil |
| Light | Breaks down light-sensitive drugs and fades colour | Amber or opaque containers, dark storage |
| Oxygen (air) | Oxidises some drugs | Fill containers well, close tightly, use antioxidants in the formula |
| pH | Many drugs are stable only in a certain pH range | Use buffers, as the formula says |
| Germs | Spoil water-based products | Hygiene, preservatives, sterile products |
| Container | The drug may react with it, stick to it, or be absorbed | Use the container recommended for the product |
| Time | The longer the storage, the more breakdown | Use FEFO, and respect expiry dates |
| Handling | Rough handling breaks packs | Careful transport |

## Shelf life and expiry

The **shelf life** is the time during which a product stays **within its quality limits** when **stored correctly**. It ends on the **expiry date** printed on the pack. A common standard is that a medicine should keep **at least 90%** of its labelled strength until expiry.

Manufacturers set shelf life through **stability studies**. In **accelerated testing**, products are stored at higher temperatures and humidity to see how quickly they break down, and the result is used to **predict** shelf life, which is then confirmed in real-time studies.

### Worked example

*A product loses 2% of its labelled strength each month, steadily. It must keep at least 90%.*

- Allowed loss = 100 − 90 = 10%
- Shelf life = 10 ÷ 2 = **5 months**

Real breakdown is not always steady, so this is a simple example only.

## Storage conditions

Typical conditions on labels:

| Description | Meaning (typical) |
|---|---|
| Store in a cool place | A cool place without direct heat |
| Store at room temperature | Normal controlled room temperature, not in direct heat |
| Store in a refrigerator | 2 to 8 °C |
| Do not freeze | Freezing damages the product |
| Protect from light | Keep in the original, light-proof pack |
| Keep dry | Keep in a tight container, away from damp |

The exact temperature limits come from the pharmacopoeia and the product label. In hot climates such as Nigeria, **a cool, shaded, ventilated store**, and a **refrigerator** for the products that need it, are important. Check the **temperature** daily with a thermometer.

## The cold chain

The **cold chain** is the system of **keeping products at the right low temperature** at every stage, from the manufacturer to the patient. It is essential for **vaccines**, **insulin**, some **antibiotics**, **biologicals** and some other products.

```
Manufacturer → Airport or port → Central cold store → State store → Local store → Health facility → Patient
```

A single break in the chain can ruin the product, and the damage is often invisible.

### Equipment

| Equipment | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Cold room | Large-scale storage at central and state level |
| Refrigerator (including ice-lined and solar types) | Storage at health facilities, at 2 to 8 °C |
| Freezer | For freezing ice packs, and for the few products stored frozen |
| Cold box | Transport of larger amounts, with ice packs |
| Vaccine carrier | Small insulated box for outreach, with ice packs |
| Ice packs | Keep the carrier cold. They must be properly conditioned before use |
| Thermometers and temperature records | Show that the temperature stayed in range |
| Vaccine vial monitor (VVM) | A label on the vial that changes colour as heat exposure builds up |

### Good practice

- Check and **record temperatures** at least **twice a day**.
- Keep vaccines **in the middle** of the refrigerator, not in the door or against the walls.
- **Do not store** food or drinks in the vaccine refrigerator.
- **Do not freeze** vaccines that must not be frozen.
- Keep a **plan** for power cuts, with spare ice packs, a generator or solar power.
- Use **FEFO**, and check VVMs.
- **Never use** a product that is suspected of having been out of range. Report it, and follow the guidance.
