## What is an ointment?

An **ointment** is a **greasy semi-solid** for application to the skin. It usually has **no water**. It is used to **soften and protect** the skin, to keep medicines in contact with the skin, and as a base for drugs.

Ointments:

- stay on the skin for a long time;
- do not wash off easily;
- keep water in the skin (they are **occlusive**), which suits dry, scaly conditions;
- are usually **not suitable for weeping or hairy areas**.

## Ointment bases

| Type | Examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrocarbon bases | White soft paraffin, yellow soft paraffin, liquid paraffin, hard paraffin | Greasy, stay on the skin, no water absorbed |
| Absorption bases | Wool fat (anhydrous lanolin), and mixtures with it | Can absorb water without losing consistency |
| Emulsifying bases | Emulsifying ointment | Mix with water, and wash off easily |
| Water-soluble bases | Macrogols (polyethylene glycols) | Wash off, no grease. Do not stay long on the skin |

**Simple ointment** is a typical base: wool fat 5%, hard paraffin 5%, cetostearyl alcohol 5% and white soft paraffin 85%. Use the formula your school gives you.

## Equipment

- A **balance** and weights (or weighing paper)
- An **ointment slab or tile** (glass or porcelain) and **spatulas** (stainless steel)
- A **dish or evaporating basin** and a **water bath**
- A **mortar** and pestle
- **Ointment jars** or tubes, and labels

Everything must be **clean and dry**.

## Method 1: Fusion (melting)

Used when the base is made of several ingredients, or when the drug dissolves in the melted base.

1. **Weigh** all the ingredients.
2. **Melt** the ingredient with the **highest melting point** first, in a dish over a water bath. Add the others in turn, **in order of descending melting point**.
3. **Heat gently**. Do not overheat, as it can spoil the base and some drugs.
4. **Remove from the heat** and **stir until cold**, so the ointment sets smooth and not grainy.
5. **Add** heat-sensitive or dissolved drugs when the base has cooled slightly, and stir until it is evenly mixed.
6. **Pack** into a jar and label.

## Method 2: Incorporation (mixing into a ready base)

Used to mix a drug into a ready-made base, without heating.

1. **Weigh** the drug and the base.
2. If the drug is a **powder**, make it **fine**.
3. Put the powder on the **tile**. Add a **small amount of base**, or of liquid paraffin, and rub with the **spatula** to a smooth paste. This is **levigation**.
4. Add the rest of the base **a little at a time**, mixing thoroughly after each addition. This is called **geometric dilution**.
5. Mix until **smooth**, with no lumps or grit, and with a **uniform colour**.
6. **Pack** firmly into the jar, with no air spaces, and label.

If the drug **dissolves** in water, dissolve it in a very small amount of water and mix with an **absorption base** such as wool fat, which can take up the water.

## Worked examples

### Percentage w/w

*Make 30 g of benzoic acid compound ointment: benzoic acid 6% w/w, salicylic acid 3% w/w, in a suitable base.*

1. Benzoic acid = 30 × 6 ÷ 100 = **1.8 g**
2. Salicylic acid = 30 × 3 ÷ 100 = **0.9 g**
3. Base = 30 − 1.8 − 0.9 = **27.3 g**

**Check.** 1.8 + 0.9 + 27.3 = **30 g**.

**Method:** levigate the two powders on the tile with a little of the base, then add the rest of the base gradually, mixing until smooth.

### Another example

*60 g of ointment containing 15% w/w zinc oxide in simple ointment.*

- Zinc oxide = 60 × 15 ÷ 100 = **9 g**
- Base = 60 − 9 = **51 g**

## Packing, labelling and storage

- Use a **wide-mouthed jar** or a **collapsible tube**, with a tight lid.
- Pack the ointment **firmly**, and smooth the top.
- Label: patient's name, name and strength, directions, **"For external use only"**, the date, and a **discard date**.
- Store in a **cool place**, because heat melts ointments.
- Advise the patient to **wash the hands** before and after use, and to apply a **thin layer**, unless told otherwise.
- A **water-free** ointment usually keeps longer than a cream, but the pharmacist decides the discard date.

## Faults and what to do

| Fault | Likely cause | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Gritty ointment | The powder was not fine enough, or not levigated | Powder and levigate again |
| Uneven colour or streaks | Poor mixing | Mix again, adding the base gradually |
| Grainy ointment after melting | Cooled too slowly without stirring | Stir until cold |
| Sticky or runny | Too much liquid, or too much heat | Use the right amounts, and heat gently |
| Air pockets in the jar | Poor packing | Pack firmly, a little at a time |
